Essay no. 001Reading the words, not just translating them

A small archive of words that resist translation.

Karāma, ṣumūd, waṭan: three Arabic words, three imperfect English equivalents—and the histories that a dictionary entry cannot carry.

Translation often begins with a practical promise: one word in one language can be replaced by another. A dictionary gives us karāma as “dignity”, ṣumūd as “steadfastness”, and waṭan as “homeland”. The entries are useful. They are not the end of the story.

Words move through institutions, slogans, songs, speeches and everyday conversations. They collect associations as they travel. A political word can carry a memory of humiliation, a claim to recognition, or a sense of belonging that its closest equivalent only partly conveys. The difficulty is not that Arabic is uniquely impossible to translate. It is that no language gives us a neutral container for history.

01 / Karāma — dignity

“Dignity” is a reasonable translation of karāma, but the word can do more than name a personal quality. In political speech, dignity may describe how a person expects to be treated by public authorities, how a community understands its worth, or what people believe has been denied to them. It links the intimate and the public: the feeling of being demeaned and the demand that institutions recognise one’s value.

That connection matters when we read accounts of protest. Material grievances—employment, prices, access to services—do not always explain why a particular moment becomes intolerable. The language of dignity can articulate an injury that is social as well as economic. It does not replace those explanations; it helps us ask what people believe is at stake.

02 / Ṣumūd — steadfastness

Ṣumūd is often rendered as “steadfastness”, “resilience” or “perseverance”. Each translation highlights something different. Resilience can sound like an individual capacity to recover; perseverance suggests continuing towards a goal. Steadfastness places more emphasis on remaining, enduring or refusing to be moved.

In Palestinian contexts, ṣumūd has acquired a specific political resonance around remaining on the land and sustaining everyday life under conditions of pressure and dispossession. Treating it as a generic synonym for resilience risks removing that context. Yet using the Arabic word without explanation can also turn it into an emblem, as if one term could summarise the experiences of an entire population. Context is not decoration: it is part of the meaning.

03 / Waṭan — homeland

Waṭan is commonly translated as “homeland” or “country”. But those English words do not always carry the same balance of territory, attachment and political imagination. A homeland can be a place one inhabits, a place one has left, or a place invoked precisely because access to it is uncertain. It may name an existing state, an imagined community, or a bond that exceeds official borders.

That ambiguity makes the word worth attending to rather than treating it as a fixed political label. When someone speaks of waṭan, the question is not only “Which country?” It may also be: what kind of belonging is being claimed, by whom, and under what conditions?

Translation as an archive

None of these words is literally untranslatable. English can describe dignity, steadfastness and homeland; Arabic speakers also disagree about meanings, uses and political implications. The point is not to protect words from translation, but to notice what translation selects—and what it leaves in the margins.

One useful method is to keep the original term visible, offer a provisional translation, and then follow its use across particular texts and situations. Who uses it? At what moment? Is it spoken by a state, a movement, a writer, a protester, or a person describing ordinary life? Does its meaning shift between a slogan and a conversation?

A word is not a shortcut to understanding a society. It is an invitation to ask better questions about the people who use it.

Perhaps the most responsible archive is therefore not a list of perfect equivalents. It is a record of choices: the word we translated, the context we kept, and the uncertainty we refused to erase.

Reading note
This essay offers an interpretive starting point, not a claim that the terms have one fixed meaning across all Arabic-speaking contexts. Their use varies by period, community and political setting. Transliteration follows a simplified scholarly form; the words are presented here as prompts for contextual reading rather than as exhaustive definitions.
