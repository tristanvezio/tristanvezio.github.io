*Between religious identity and the boundaries of secular citizenship*

![Sultan Mosque, Singapore](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Sultan_Mosque_Singapore1.jpg)

*Photo: Philip Nalangan, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.*

When I lived in Singapore, I was staying directly opposite the Sultan Mosque (Masjid Sultan), in the historic district of Kampong Glam, in the Bugis area. I would hear the muezzin's call to prayer from where I lived. It was part of the rhythm of everyday life, a familiar sound connecting the surrounding streets to a religious tradition deeply rooted in the city.

What struck me was the context in which this sound existed. Singapore is not an officially Muslim country. It is a secular state, home to several religious communities, and yet the call to prayer was not hidden away from public life. It could be heard, quite naturally, in the middle of the city.

That experience raised a question that has stayed with me: what does it mean for Islam to be so visibly and audibly present in a country whose political identity is not founded on religion? How does a secular state accommodate a religious community with its own institutions, traditions and forms of authority?

Singapore offers an interesting setting in which to explore these questions. Its approach to secularism does not simply remove religion from public life. Instead, it recognises and regulates religious communities within a broader framework of multiculturalism and national cohesion.

## A secular state, a recognised religion

Since independence in 1965, Singapore has built its national identity around a model of multiculturalism and religious pluralism. Its population includes Chinese, Malay, Indian and other communities, as well as a range of religious traditions. The state does not establish one religion as the foundation of national identity. Rather, it seeks to maintain a common civic framework in which different communities can coexist.

Yet religion is far from absent from public life. Mosques, churches, temples, religious schools and community organisations are part of the country's social landscape. Islam, in particular, has a recognised institutional position.

The Administration of Muslim Law Act (AMLA), enacted in 1966 and brought into effect in 1968, established the legal framework for the administration of Muslim affairs. It also provided for the Islamic Religious Council of Singapore, known as MUIS (Majlis Ugama Islam Singapura).

MUIS is responsible for a wide range of matters, including zakat, waqf, halal certification, mosque administration, pilgrimage affairs and Islamic education. It also provides religious guidance and advises the President on matters relating to Islam. Madrasahs, meanwhile, combine Islamic education with the requirements of the national education system.

This arrangement gives Islam an established place within Singapore's public institutions. At the same time, it places religious affairs within a framework defined by state legislation and administrative oversight. The relationship is therefore more complex than a simple separation between religion and government: the state recognises Islam while also helping shape the conditions under which it is organised and practised.

## Muslim identity beyond a single experience

Understanding Islam in Singapore also requires attention to the relationship between religion, ethnicity and citizenship.

The Malay community has historically formed the largest part of Singapore's Muslim population. Malay identity and Islam are consequently closely associated in the country's social landscape, although they should not be treated as interchangeable. Singapore's Muslim population also includes people from other ethnic and national backgrounds.

Religious belonging intersects with language, ethnicity, class, gender, citizenship and migration status. The experience of a Singaporean Muslim citizen, for example, may differ considerably from that of a migrant Muslim worker.

In his 2023 article on Tablighi Jamaat and the transnationalisation of Islam in Singapore, geographer Orlando Woods examines how religious networks extend beyond national borders. His work draws attention to the ways in which transnational religious connections complicate the state's understanding of the secular citizen, particularly in relation to Bangladeshi migrant workers.

This perspective is important because it reminds us that Islam in Singapore cannot be understood as a single, uniform experience. The relationship between religious identity and national belonging is shaped not only by faith, but also by an individual's position within society and their relationship with the state.

## The tudung debate: the limits of religious visibility

The boundaries of religious expression become particularly visible in debates surrounding the tudung, or Muslim headscarf.

For many Muslim women, wearing the tudung is an expression of faith and personal identity. In Singapore, however, its use in certain professional and institutional settings has long been debated.

One prominent example concerned Muslim nurses working in the public healthcare sector. For many years, they were not permitted to wear the tudung with their uniforms. The issue raised questions about religious freedom, professional neutrality and the state's understanding of a common public identity.

In August 2021, Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong announced that female Muslim staff in the public healthcare sector would be allowed to wear the tudung with their uniforms from 1 November 2021, if they wished to do so. The change applied to more than 7,000 female Muslim uniformed staff in the sector. It did not, however, extend to all public services: restrictions remained in other uniformed institutions, including the armed forces and police.

The decision marked a significant change, but it also illustrated the limits of religious accommodation. The government justified different rules across institutions by referring to considerations such as uniformity, institutional neutrality and operational requirements.

Research by Humairah Zainal and George Wong, in their 2017 article *Voices behind the Veil*, offers another perspective on this debate. Drawing on the lived experiences of Malay-Muslim women, including nurses, the authors examine how women negotiate their religious identities within professional environments.

Their work highlights an important dimension often overlooked in public discussions: the debate is not simply about the relationship between religion and the state. It is also about individual choices, professional aspirations and the everyday experiences of Muslim women themselves. Not all Muslim women relate to the tudung in the same way, and their experiences should not be reduced to a single narrative.

The policy change of 2021 therefore illustrates both movement and continuity: greater accommodation of religious expression in one public sector, alongside restrictions that remain elsewhere.

## Hearing Islam within a secular society

Returning to the sound of the muezzin in Kampong Glam, what first appeared to be a simple contrast reveals something more complex about Singapore.

The call to prayer was not an exception to the country's secular identity. It existed alongside it. The presence of mosques, the institutional organisation of Muslim affairs through MUIS and the debates surrounding the tudung all point to a relationship in which Islam is recognised as part of society, while its public and institutional expression remains shaped by the state.

Singapore shows that secularism does not necessarily require religion to disappear from public life. It can also involve the active organisation of religious diversity, the recognition of certain institutions and the negotiation of boundaries around religious expression.

Living opposite the Sultan Mosque gave me a small, everyday glimpse of this relationship. The call to prayer was part of the city I inhabited, even though Islam did not define the state in which that city existed. Looking back, that coexistence captures something essential about Singapore: the relationship between religion and the nation is not defined by the absence of one or the dominance of the other, but by the ongoing negotiation of their place in public life.
